The Elizabethan Court of Requests

Places named in the pleadings of REQ 2, c. 1558–1603, across the historic counties of England and Wales — read out of the catalogue descriptions themselves, not just the county field.

An experiment — and some of these places are wrong.

Nobody checked this map before you saw it. That is the point of the demonstration, and it is also the problem with it. Two examples found by clicking on it: “the Court of Admiralty”, mapped to a quarry in Dorset; and [CSP] — the catalogue’s shorthand for the Calendar of State Papers, cited whenever it vouches for a man’s court office — mapped to the Chartered Society of Physiotherapy in London, where it briefly became the sixth-busiest place in Tudor litigation. Both are fixed. Both were invisible in the totals and obvious on the page.

Errors of that kind are not a defect in the idea; they are the reason the Workbench is being built. A machine can find candidate places and narrow them down. Choosing between the candidates is a job for someone who knows the county — and giving that person good tools is the actual problem worth solving.

    Named but not located in

    Names the extractor found that WHG’s index could not match inside this county. Matching is strict — a name must match a record exactly — so this list holds three kinds of thing: real places the gazetteer does not yet know, names the sources spell differently from the index, and people, which is what searching inside a county is for. Loosening the match to catch the first two reliably placed the third, so it was not kept.

      How the Workbench helps. It stops for a person wherever you want it to: its review stage puts the rival candidates for an ambiguous name side by side, takes a rejection, lets you search by hand for one the index missed, and records why you decided as you did — and a decision survives a re-run of the extraction. Every place on this map skipped that step. Anyone publishing from it would not, and the not-located list above is where they would start.
